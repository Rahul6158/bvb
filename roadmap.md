I’ll use the uploaded learning brief as the structure for this course. Its central goal is to understand **what is actually happening underneath the surface when someone buys or sells a financial asset**, rather than memorizing strategies or terminology.

We will **not** cover the whole curriculum at once. Each lesson will introduce a small connected concept, give examples and exercises, test your understanding, and only then move forward.

# Lesson 1 — What Is a Financial Market?

Before we discuss charts, candles, brokers, or strategies, we need to answer a much more fundamental question:

> **What is a financial market?**

## 1. What is it?

At its simplest:

**A financial market is a system through which people and organizations exchange financial assets.**

For example:

* Someone wants to sell shares.
* Someone else wants to buy those shares.
* The market provides a mechanism for bringing those two sides together.

But this definition isn't enough.

We need to understand **why such a system needs to exist in the first place.**

---

# 2. Why does a financial market exist?

Imagine there were no financial markets.

You own a company.

Your company needs ₹10 crore to expand.

Another person has ₹10 crore and wants their money to potentially grow.

There is a potential exchange:

**You:**
"I need capital."

**Investor:**
"I have capital and want an investment opportunity."

But how do you find each other?

How do you determine a reasonable price?

How do you transfer ownership?

How do you record the transaction?

How do you later sell that investment?

A financial market provides infrastructure and rules that help solve these problems.

So one fundamental purpose of financial markets is:

> **Connecting people who have capital with people or organizations that need capital, while providing mechanisms for valuation, trading, and transfer of financial claims.**

There is another important function:

### Liquidity

Suppose you bought an asset yesterday.

You don't necessarily want to hold it forever.

You may want to sell it today.

A functioning market can provide other participants willing to trade with you.

That ability to convert an asset into cash relatively easily is related to **liquidity**.

We'll eventually go very deep into liquidity because it becomes central to understanding actual price movement.

---

# 3. What is actually being exchanged?

This is where many beginners initially misunderstand markets.

Suppose you buy a company's stock.

You are not buying:

* the company's office
* its computers
* its products
* its employees

You are purchasing a **financial claim representing ownership in the company**.

Similarly, when you buy a bond, you generally aren't buying a piece of the company.

You are acquiring a **debt claim**.

Different financial instruments represent different kinds of claims, rights, or contracts.

We'll study each one separately later.

---

# 4. A simple mental model

Think of a financial market like a huge organized marketplace.

A normal marketplace might have:

**Seller → Marketplace → Buyer**

A financial market has something similar:

**Seller of financial asset → Market infrastructure → Buyer**

But unlike a vegetable market, financial markets have sophisticated systems for:

* determining prices
* matching orders
* recording ownership
* managing risk
* clearing transactions
* settling transactions

That is why later we'll study the chain:

**Order → Matching → Execution → Clearing → Settlement**

---

# 5. Where does trading enter?

Suppose you own one share.

You bought it for:

**₹100**

Later, another participant is willing to buy it for:

**₹110**

You sell.

Ignoring costs:

**Purchase price = ₹100**
**Sale price = ₹110**

Your gross gain:

**₹110 − ₹100 = ₹10**

That is the basic numerical mechanism behind a long trade.

But notice something important:

**The market itself didn't magically give you ₹10.**

Someone else purchased the asset from you for ₹110.

So immediately we have a deeper question:

> **Why was someone willing to pay ₹110 when you had paid ₹100?**

That question leads us toward:

**Price formation → expectations → information → supply/demand → liquidity → order flow**

And that is one of the major themes of this course.

---

# 6. The first important distinction

There are two concepts we should keep separate.

### Investing

Generally involves acquiring an asset with the intention of benefiting from its longer-term economic characteristics and potential returns.

### Trading

Generally focuses more directly on buying and selling financial instruments to attempt to profit from changes in their prices or other market characteristics.

These can overlap.

A person holding a stock for several years is making investment decisions.

A person buying and selling the same stock within minutes is trading.

But **time horizon alone isn't the complete distinction**. We'll examine that properly later.

---

# 7. One thing I want you to remember

Don't think of a market as:

> "A place where prices go up and down so traders can make money."

That's looking at the market from the trader's perspective.

Instead, start with:

> **A financial market is an organized mechanism that allows participants to exchange financial claims and transfer capital, ownership, and risk.**

Trading happens **inside that system**.
